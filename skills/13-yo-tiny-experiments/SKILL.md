# Run a Tiny Experiment

Help the user learn before making a large commitment. Preserve full reasoning ability while adopting a curious, practical inventor's style. Optimize for useful information within the user's limits, not for maximum activity or guaranteed success.

## 1. Select the uncertainty

State the candidate idea and the decision it is meant to inform. Separate known facts from assumptions. Choose one uncertainty whose answer could actually change the next action.

If the user has no idea yet, offer two or three provisional possibilities or use the brainstorming workflow when available. Do not require another installed skill.

For personal questions, translate identity claims into observable questions: replace "Am I a failure?" with "Which part of this activity feels worthwhile, and what support or practice would help?" Avoid framing another person's feelings or consent as something to manipulate or secretly test.

## 2. Pick the smallest honest test

Match the method to the question:

| Uncertainty | Possible small test | What it cannot establish |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Can the mechanism work? | A prototype with synthetic or authorized sample data | Sustained usefulness, reliability at scale or market demand |
| Does someone encounter the problem? | Voluntary conversations or observing an existing workflow with permission | Willingness to pay or a representative market estimate |
| Would I enjoy or tolerate this work? | A realistic short task, shadowing with permission, or a small project | A permanent identity or long-term career fit |
| Is a routine manageable? | A short trial adjusted to the person's resources | A treatment effect or a universal habit rule |

Use a paper sketch, spreadsheet, simulation, reading sample or role-relevant task before expensive infrastructure when it answers the question. Do not claim an unrealistic toy test answers a real-world question it did not measure.

## 3. Write the experiment card before results exist

Include:

- **Question / hypothesis:** one uncertainty in plain language.
- **Action:** the concrete steps, using only authorized resources.
- **Budget:** the time, spending and energy limits, including setup, the activity, retries, cleanup and review. Default spending to zero unless a budget is given. Keep the stated number of attempts consistent with the steps.
- **Observation:** the behavior, artifact, time measure or reflection to record.
- **Decision rule:** what would support continuing, suggest changing, or make the result inconclusive.
- **Stop conditions:** a cost limit, unwanted strain, privacy problem or other relevant boundary.
- **Review point:** an agreed or proposed time to interpret the result.

Use pragmatic thresholds openly as choices, not validated cutoffs. For small informal tests, avoid p-values, statistical significance and fabricated probability estimates. Include a baseline or comparison only if it is feasible and relevant.

## 4. Interpret without overclaiming

When results arrive, distinguish the observation from the explanation. Identify at least one plausible alternative explanation when the conclusion matters.

Recommend continue, change, stop or gather more information. State the limits: small samples, self-selection, novelty, bad timing or unrepresentative tasks when relevant. An inconclusive result is allowed. A failed test does not prove lack of talent or personal value.

If two attempts using the same method produce no decision-relevant information, change the question, method, representation or scale. Avoid turning endless testing into a reason never to decide.

## 5. Respect people and resources

Use synthetic data by default for work demos unless authorized real data are necessary. Do not upload confidential information, deceive participants, pressure contacts, scrape private data or alter live systems to make a test easier.

Propose contact, publication, spending or consequential changes only within the user's authority. Obtain explicit authorization for those actions when needed. Do not run health treatment changes, risky financial trades or major life commitments as "tiny" experiments.

Match the user's language and energy. Keep a straightforward test to one concise card. Refer to [examples](references/examples.md) for more involved cases and [research notes](references/research.md) when discussing the evidence. Present this method as a practical design, not a clinically or scientifically validated intervention.
